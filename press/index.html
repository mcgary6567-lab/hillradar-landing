Where the data comes from

Every record traces to a primary government source. House members file Periodic Transaction Reports with the Clerk of the House; senators file through the Senate's electronic Financial Disclosure (eFD) system. Names, parties, chambers and states come from the official congress-legislators directory. We publish the filing date and disclosure delay on every row so the provenance of each figure is visible.

How we build it

  • We read the official House and Senate disclosure records several times a day and add every new transaction.
  • Filings are parsed into structured fields, with company names matched to tickers and the congressional directory for lawmaker details.
  • Amendments add a new filing rather than replacing one, so records are de-duplicated by lawmaker, asset, date and amount.
  • For each trade we compute the disclosure delay (filing date minus trade date); the median across the record is 27 days.

Known limitations

We state these plainly because they matter to anyone reporting on the data:

  • Amounts are ranges, not exact figures (e.g. $15,001–$50,000); 69% of transactions fall in the smallest band.
  • A built-in lag: the law allows up to 45 days to file, and 14.6% of filings still arrive after that.
  • Source quality varies: older filings are scanned paper requiring OCR, the main source of parsing errors in any tracker.
  • Filers can amend reports after the fact, so figures can move slightly.
  • Widely diversified mutual funds and ETFs are often exempt from Periodic Transaction Reports.

Data points on every trade

Lawmaker, chamber, party and state; company or asset and ticker; transaction type (purchase, sale or exchange); trade date and filing date; reported amount range; disclosure delay; and the owner (self, spouse, joint or dependent child).
